Collaboration metrics

Field Field Value
Name Collaboration metrics
Can Apply To An individual researcher's body of work, a research group, or an institution.
Metric Definition Collaboration metrics describe the extent and reach of an entity's research partnerships, based on the co-authorship affiliations recorded on its publications. They are typically reported at three geographic levels — institutional (co-authors at the same institution), national (co-authors at other institutions in the same country), and international (co-authors affiliated with institutions in other countries) — and can also be reported as academic–corporate collaboration, where a co-author is affiliated with industry, government, or an NGO rather than an academic institution.
Metric Calculation For a given set of publications, each output is classified by the geographic and sectoral spread of its co-authors' affiliations. The number and percentage of publications with institutional, national, or international co-authorship is then reported. Impact of collaboration is usually reported alongside these counts as the average Field Weighted Citation Impact (FWCI) for each collaboration category, allowing comparison of, for example, the citation impact of internationally co-authored papers versus single-institution papers.
Data Sources SciVal and Scopus (Elsevier) are the most commonly used sources for these figures in Australian universities, drawing on author affiliation metadata recorded in Scopus. InCites and Web of Science (Clarivate) provide comparable collaboration analysis using Web of Science affiliation data.
Appropriate Use Cases Useful for demonstrating the breadth of a researcher's professional network and their capacity to work across institutions, sectors, and borders — relevant evidence in promotion, grant, and fellowship applications. Collaboration data can also help identify existing and potential research partners, and institutions use it to track progress against strategic collaboration goals.
Limitations Collaboration metrics depend on the accuracy and completeness of author affiliation metadata in the underlying database, which can be inconsistent, especially for researchers who have moved institutions or hold multiple affiliations. Metrics start to become meaningful only with a reasonable volume of publications (a handful of papers can produce a misleadingly high or low collaboration percentage). Rates of collaboration also vary significantly by discipline and career stage, so comparisons across fields or between early-career and senior researchers should be made with caution.
Inappropriate Use Cases A high proportion of collaborative publications should not, by itself, be read as an indicator of research quality or leadership — collaboration norms differ widely by field (e.g. large multi-author consortia in some sciences versus predominantly single- or dual-author work in the humanities). It should not be used to compare researchers across disciplines without accounting for this variation.
Available Metric Sources SciVal and Scopus (Elsevier), InCites and Web of Science (Clarivate).
Transparency The classification of a publication's collaboration level is based on recorded author affiliations, which are verifiable but subject to metadata quality issues (e.g. inconsistent institution naming, missing affiliations).
Website N/A
Timeframe Collaboration metrics can be calculated over any publication window (e.g. last 10 years, career total) and are typically updated as new publications are indexed.
