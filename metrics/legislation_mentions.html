Legislation and parliamentary mentions

This metric is closely related to Policy mentions, which covers citations in government and NGO policy documents more broadly. This page covers the narrower, and often more prized, evidence of a research output being referenced directly in parliamentary proceedings or legislative debate — for example a mention in Hansard, the official transcript of parliamentary debate.

Field Field Value
Name Legislation and parliamentary mentions
Can Apply To Individual research outputs, most commonly journal articles and reports, though books and other output types can also be cited.
Metric Definition A count of instances where a research output is directly referenced in parliamentary proceedings — including debate transcripts (e.g. Hansard), committee reports, or bills — indicating the research has informed or been cited in the process of law-making or parliamentary scrutiny.
Metric Calculation A simple count of the number of parliamentary sources (debates, committee reports, bills) that cite a given output, or the number of outputs by an author cited across parliamentary sources. Providers that track this typically search the full text of parliamentary records for citations, references, or explicit mentions of a publication.
Data Sources Overton (a policy document search engine that indexes parliamentary transcripts, including Hansard, alongside broader government and NGO policy documents) is the most widely used commercial source. National parliamentary websites (e.g. the Australian Parliament's Hansard search) can also be searched directly, though this is manual and time-consuming.
Appropriate Use Cases Strong evidence of real-world policy relevance and influence, particularly valuable in promotion and impact case studies where demonstrating societal reach beyond academia is required. A direct parliamentary mention is generally considered stronger evidence of influence than a citation in a general policy document, since it shows the research reached the point of legislative or parliamentary consideration.
Limitations Coverage depends heavily on the tracking source — Overton and similar tools do not capture every jurisdiction or every parliamentary record, and coverage of non-English-language parliaments is typically weaker. A mention does not indicate whether the research was used to support or oppose a position, or how significant its influence was on the eventual outcome; it only confirms that it was referenced.
Inappropriate Use Cases Should not be treated as a measure of research quality, nor assumed to reflect the degree of influence — a passing mention and a research finding that directly shaped a policy recommendation both count equally as "one mention" unless the context is examined and reported alongside the count.
Available Metric Sources Overton; CRO (CSU Research Output, where researchers have manually recorded evidence of policy or parliamentary influence); national parliamentary Hansard search tools.
Transparency Parliamentary records themselves are public and independently verifiable. Commercial tracking tools such as Overton do not publish a complete list of every source indexed, so absence of a result should not be read as confirmation that no mention exists.
Website Overton
Timeframe Mentions can occur at any point after publication and are typically tracked on an ongoing basis as new parliamentary records are indexed.
